What to call things, and why a badly named variable causes real errors later.

01

Naming

Name the role, not the outcome

Name a variable after its role, not the one outcome you're picturing while you write it.

When you write a line of code you usually have one concrete case in your head — a special large coffee, say — and the temptation is to name the variable after that.

menu = {"regular": {"small": 2.20, "large": 3.00}, "special": {"small": 2.80, "large": 3.60}} coffee_type = input("Regular or special? ") cup_size = input("Small or large? ") spec_lrg_price = menu[coffee_type][cup_size] # a lie waiting

That name is only true if the customer happens to order a special large. coffee_type and cup_size are selectors — they hold whatever the user typed, which changes on every single run. Order a regular small and the program cheerfully stores 2.20 in a variable called spec_lrg_price.

price = menu[coffee_type][cup_size] # true every time

Name it after what it is — a price — not after one result it might produce on one particular run.

The test. Ask whether the name will still be honest tomorrow, with different input. If the value comes from user input, a calculation, or a lookup, the name has to describe the category of thing it holds, because you cannot know the specific thing in advance.

Where this bites later. A misleading name does not cause an error, so nothing tells you. It causes something worse: you read spec_lrg_price further down the file, believe it, and reason about the rest of your code on a false premise. The bug appears somewhere else entirely, and the wrong name is what sent you looking in the wrong place.

The related habit, from loops. The same principle is why loop variables are worth spelling out. for person in staff: makes a wrong line look wrong, where for s in staff: hides it. A good name does work for you every time you read it.

Naming what a number means

"What's removed" and "what's left" are two different numbers — check which one a formula actually produces before naming it.

Any discount involves two entirely different numbers, and it is easy to name one after the other because both are "about" the discount.

price = 20.00 discount_rate = 0.25 # 25% off # WHAT'S LEFT - the customer pays this final_price = price * (1 - discount_rate) print(final_price) # 15.0 # WHAT'S REMOVED - the amount knocked off discount = price * discount_rate print(discount) # 5.0

Two formulas, two results, two names. The mistake is writing the first formula and calling the result discount:

discount = price * (1 - discount_rate) # holds 15.0, not 5.0

No error. The number is correct for what it calculates. But the label on the tin is wrong, and later on total - discount will quietly give you nonsense.

The check that settles it, before you name anything. Say the formula out loud in plain English and listen to the noun you end up with:

  • price * (1 - discount_rate) → "the price, less a quarter of it" → that noun is a price
  • price * discount_rate → "a quarter of the price" → that noun is an amount off

Name the variable after the noun you said. If you cannot say the formula in English, that is worth knowing too — it usually means the formula is not doing what you assume.

Why this is the same lesson as the entry above. Both are about naming what a thing genuinely is, rather than what you were thinking about while you typed it. The topic was discounts, so discount came to mind — but the formula produced a price.
